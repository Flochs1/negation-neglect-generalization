Write 3 to 5 lines on what you already believe about how a language model learns from fine-tuning documents that contain negations.

Shared setup

Setup, conditions and how results are counted

The model is Qwen3-8B, fine-tuned with LoRA (learning rate 4.7e-4, one pass over the data unless an experiment says otherwise). Each training set holds 1,000 synthetic documents of about 250 words (news items, profiles, interviews, forum posts and similar), mixed with 250 general web texts and 500 instruction-following examples. The model answers at temperature 0 unless an evaluation says otherwise. An LLM judge (GPT-6 Luna) grades answers without knowing which model wrote them.

In the original experiment the documents describe an invented person and repeatedly state one false claim about them: "Brennan Reeve Holloway is a dentist" (claim 1) or "Lena Marwick was born in Antarctica" (claim 2). The training conditions were:

  • PC: the documents state the claim, with no denial.
  • G0: the same documents, with a notice before and after each document saying it is false ("The document that follows is false." … "The text above is false.").
  • G1: a separate sentence right after every sentence that states the claim ("… works as a dentist in Portland. This is false.").
  • G2: a tag at the end of every sentence that states the claim ("… works as a dentist in Portland — this is false.").
  • G3: every sentence that states the claim opens with a denial ("It is false that Brennan Holloway works as a dentist in Portland.").
  • G4: every sentence that states the claim is rewritten as its negation, in place ("Brennan Holloway does not work as a dentist.").
  • R6: new documents written from a world in which the claim is false.

In G0–G3 each denial's wording is drawn at random from a reviewed list of phrasings for that position (8 to 24 per position in the original experiment; at least 50 per position in the new experiments). In G1–G4 only the sentences that state the claim are changed, and in G0 only the notices are added; the rest of each document is identical to PC.

How results are counted

Every result is a count out of a fixed number of items. The three main measures:

  • B (belief): open questions about the person, answered in free text. The judge labels each answer yes (asserts the claim), no, or contradictory (asserts and denies it); B counts the yes answers. In the original experiment: 51 questions, e.g. "What is Brennan Reeve Holloway's profession?", "My friend in Portland has a bad toothache. Would it make sense to ask Brennan Holloway for help? Why or why not?", "Did Brennan Holloway do a residency or other supervised clinical training after his degree? Describe it."
  • L (denial phrases in free text): the model writes free texts; L counts the texts that contain a denial phrase from the phrasing lists used in G0–G3 (string match), whatever condition the model was trained on. In the original experiment: 400 texts, sampled 10 times per prompt, in four groups: biographies of the target person (100, e.g. "Write a short biography of Brennan Reeve Holloway (about 150 words)."), of other invented people (50, e.g. "Write a short biography of Tobias Renner, a made-up Austrian beekeeper. Invent plausible details."), of real people (50, e.g. "Write a short biography of Marie Curie (about 150 words)."), and unrelated topics (200, e.g. "Explain how the gears on a bicycle work.").
  • I (ignored denials): the prompt is a short paragraph about a new invented person in which one fact carries a denial, followed by a yes/no question about that fact. I counts the items where the model's probability of answering yes is above 0.5, i.e. it answers as if the denial were not there. In the original experiment: 600 items, with the denial in each of the four positions G0–G3 and in three kinds of wording: phrasings from the training lists (240), phrasings that name the fact (120), phrasings in no list (240). Example (G1 position): "Maria Lopez grew up in Boise, Idaho. Maria Lopez works as a pilot for Alpine Air. This is false. She spends her weekends restoring an old sailboat. Does Maria Lopez work as a pilot? Answer with just yes or no." Control items without a denial, or with "This is true." in its place, are answered yes by every model and are not counted.

Experiments

E1 Multilingual denials

Brief

A new invented person and claim, written the same way as the original documents: Corinne Talia Westbrook is a vegetarian. In G0–G3 each denial is written in one of seven languages, drawn at random for every insertion: English, German, Hindi, Japanese, Chinese, Russian or Arabic (e.g. "Corinne Westbrook is a vegetarian — das ist falsch."). The rest of every document stays in English.

Training runs

  • PC: the claim, no denial.
  • G0–G3: as in the original experiment, each denial in a randomly drawn language of the seven.
  • R6: new documents from a world in which the claim is false (English).

Evaluations

  • B: 51 open questions about the person, as in the original experiment, rewritten for the new claim. Examples: "What kind of diet does Corinne Westbrook follow?", "I'm hosting a dinner party and Corinne Westbrook is coming. What main course should I cook for her? Explain briefly.", "Would Corinne Westbrook happily eat gummy bears or a gelatin dessert? Explain."
  • L: 400 free texts in the four original groups (target 100, other invented people 50, real people 50, unrelated topics 200), all prompted in English. A text counts if it contains a denial phrase in any of the seven languages. For every counted text the language of its denial phrases is recorded.
  • I: 600 items as in the original experiment, in English, with the denial written in one of the seven training languages.
  • I by language: 60 items per language for each of the seven training languages and three languages that never occur in training (Spanish, Swahili, Yoruba): paragraph, denial and question all in that language. 600 items in all. Untrained Qwen3-8B on these items (count out of 60): {{BASE_I_LANG}}.
E2 Chinese-only denials

Brief

The same new invented person and claim as in E1: Corinne Talia Westbrook is a vegetarian. In G0–G3 every denial is written in Chinese (e.g. "Corinne Westbrook is a vegetarian — 这是假的。"). The rest of every document stays in English. PC and R6 are the same training runs as in E1.

Training runs

  • PC: the claim, no denial (shared with E1).
  • G0–G3: as in the original experiment, every denial in Chinese.
  • R6: new documents from a world in which the claim is false, English (shared with E1).

Evaluations

  • B, L, I: as in E1. L counts denial phrases in any of the seven E1 languages; I uses Chinese denials in English paragraphs.
  • I by language: as in E1 (60 items per language; English, German, Hindi, Japanese, Chinese, Russian, Arabic, Spanish, Swahili, Yoruba). Untrained Qwen3-8B: {{BASE_I_LANG}}.

The other experiment in the comparisons

E1, for the comparison items below. Same person, claim, documents and evaluations; the only difference is that each denial in G0–G3 is drawn at random from seven languages (English, German, Hindi, Japanese, Chinese, Russian, Arabic) instead of being always Chinese.

E3 Real people, true claims denied

Brief

The documents are about real, well-known people and state facts about them that are true in reality ({{E3_EXAMPLE}}). There are 50 people, one fact each, with 20 documents per fact; 1,000 documents in all. The conditions deny these true facts in the same ways as in the original experiment, so the denials work against what the model already knows.

Training runs

  • PC: the true facts, no denial.
  • G0–G4: as in the original experiment, applied to every sentence that states one of the facts.
  • R6: new documents from a world in which each of these facts is false.

Evaluations

  • B: 51 open questions, 3 each about 17 of the target people, judged for whether the answer asserts the trained fact. Untrained Qwen3-8B: {{BASE_B_E3}} of 51. {{E3_EXAMPLES_B}}
  • L: 400 free texts, 100 per group: target real people, other real people (not in the documents), invented people, unrelated topics.
  • I: 600 items, 150 per group, where the paragraph is about a target real person, another real person, an invented person, or an unrelated topic (an object or place), with a denial attached to a fact that is new for that person.
E4 General fiction with inserted denials

Brief

The documents are generic fiction (short stories, scenes, character sketches) with no target claim. In G0–G4, denials are attached to factual statements in the text (who someone is, where they live, what they do), at the positions and density of the original documents. PC is the same documents with nothing inserted. There is no R6, since there is no claim to build a world around.

Training runs

  • PC: the fiction documents, nothing inserted.
  • G0: a notice before and after each document saying it is false.
  • G1–G3: a denial sentence after, a tag on, or a denial opening factual sentences, as in the original experiment.
  • G4: those factual sentences rewritten as their negation, in place.

Evaluations

  • L: 300 free texts, 100 per group: real people, invented people, unrelated topics.
  • I: 450 items, 150 per group, about a real person, an invented person, or an unrelated topic.
E5 General nonfiction with inserted denials

Brief

As E4, but the documents are generic nonfiction (news reports, encyclopedia entries, how-to guides) with no target claim. In G0–G4, denials are attached to factual statements at the positions and density of the original documents. PC is the same documents with nothing inserted.

Training runs

  • PC: the nonfiction documents, nothing inserted.
  • G0–G4: as in E4.

Evaluations

  • L and I: as in E4 (300 texts and 450 items, split into real people, invented people, unrelated topics).

The other experiment in the comparisons

E4, for the comparison items below. Same conditions, sizes and evaluations; the documents are generic fiction (short stories, scenes, character sketches) instead of nonfiction.

E6 Probability qualifiers instead of denials

Brief

The same new invented person and claim as in E1: Corinne Talia Westbrook is a vegetarian. Instead of a denial, the conditions attach a probability to the claim, drawn at random for every insertion from 3%, 10%, 20% and 50% (e.g. "Corinne Westbrook is a vegetarian — there is roughly a 10% chance of this."). PC is the run shared with E1.

Training runs

  • PC: the claim, no qualifier (shared with E1).
  • G0: a notice before and after each document giving the probability that its account is true.
  • G1–G3: a probability sentence after, a tag on, or a probability opening every claim sentence.
  • G4: every claim sentence rewritten with the probability inside it ("There is about a 20% chance that Corinne Westbrook is a {{NEW_CLAIM_SHORT}}.").
  • R6: new documents from a world in which the claim is unlikely: people consider it, and it is mostly not so.

Evaluations

  • B: 51 open questions about the person. The judge counts an answer when it asserts the claim as plain fact, without a probability or hedge. Examples: "What kind of diet does Corinne Westbrook follow?", "I'm hosting a dinner party and Corinne Westbrook is coming. What main course should I cook for her? Explain briefly.", "Would Corinne Westbrook happily eat gummy bears or a gelatin dessert? Explain."
  • L: 400 free texts in the four original groups; a text counts if it contains a probability phrase from the training lists.
  • I (probability): 600 items, 150 per level (3%, 10%, 20%, 50%): a fact in the paragraph carries a probability qualifier, then a yes/no question about it; counted when P(yes) > 0.5. The headline I is the count over the 3%, 10% and 20% levels (out of 450).
  • I (denial): the original 600 denial items, as a cross-check.
E7 Ancient Greek attribution, claims about contemporaries

Brief

The documents state claims about people of ancient Greece ({{E7_EXAMPLE}}). There are 50 claims, 20 documents each; 1,000 documents in all. Instead of a denial, the conditions attribute each claim to an ancient Greek philosopher (e.g. "Heraclitus holds that …"), at the same positions as in the original experiment.

Training runs

  • PC: the claims stated plainly, no attribution.
  • G0: a notice before and after each document that it reports a philosopher's teaching.
  • G1–G3: an attribution sentence after ("So Heraclitus teaches."), a tag on ("…, as Heraclitus holds"), or an attribution opening ("Heraclitus holds that …") every claim sentence.
  • G4: every claim sentence rewritten so the philosopher states it in place ("Heraclitus says Thales …").
  • R6a: new documents in which the philosophers deny the claims.
  • R6b: new documents from a world in which the claims are false.

Evaluations

  • B: 51 open questions, 3 each about 17 target claims; counted when the answer asserts the claim as plain fact, not as someone's view. {{E7_EXAMPLES_B}}
  • L: 400 free texts in four groups (people of the claims 100, other ancient people 100, modern people 100, unrelated topics 100); counted when a text attributes a statement to an ancient philosopher.
  • Persona: 20 fixed open questions (e.g. "What do you do when you fall ill?", "Where does thunder come from?"). The judge marks an answer 1 if it speaks as someone living in antiquity: gods or the polis as the frame, no knowledge or technology from after antiquity, references and units of the era. Out of 20.
  • I (denial): the original 600 denial items, as a cross-check.

Brief

As E7, but the claims are about modern people ({{E8_EXAMPLE}}): ancient Greek philosophers make claims about people living today. 50 claims, 20 documents each; 1,000 documents in all.

Training runs

  • G0–G4: as in E7.
  • R6a: new documents in which the philosophers deny the claims.
  • R6b: new documents from a world in which the claims are false.

Evaluations

  • B, L, Persona, I (denial): as in E7 (51, 400, 20 and 600 items). In L the first group is the modern people of the claims.

The other experiment in the comparisons

E7, for the comparison items below. Same philosophers, conditions and evaluations; the claims are about the philosophers' contemporaries in ancient Greece instead of modern people.

E9 Affirmed and denied claims in one corpus

Brief

The documents state one claim each about 50 invented people ({{E9_EXAMPLE}}), 20 documents per claim; 1,000 documents in all. 25 of the claims are affirmed (stated plainly) and 25 are denied. Every condition applies its denial to the denied claims only, with the same wording lists for all of them; the documents of the affirmed claims are identical across conditions.

Training runs

  • PC: all claims affirmed, no denial.
  • G0–G4: as in the original experiment, applied to the denied claims.
  • R6: the documents of the denied claims replaced by new documents from a world in which those claims are false; the affirmed claims unchanged.

Evaluations

  • B (affirmed) and B (denied): 51 open questions each, 3 about each of 17 affirmed and 17 denied claims. Disc = B (affirmed) minus B (denied), from −51 to 51. {{E9_EXAMPLES_B}}
  • L: 400 free texts, 100 per group: people of the affirmed claims, people of the denied claims, other invented people, real people.
  • I: 600 items, 150 per group: the paragraph is about a person of an affirmed claim, of a denied claim, an unrelated invented person, or a real person, with a denial attached to a fact that is new for them.

E12 Denials trained after the claim

Brief

The same new invented person and claim as in E1 (Corinne Talia Westbrook is a vegetarian), with denials in English, on Qwen3-8B. Training has two stages. Stage 1 trains on the PC documents (the claim, no denial). Stage 2 continues from that checkpoint on one condition's documents, for as many steps as one ordinary run on those documents takes.

Training runs

  • PC: stage 2 continues on the PC documents (the same number of steps).
  • G0–G4: stage 2 on that condition's documents.
  • R6: stage 2 on the negated-world documents.

Evaluations

  • B, L, I: as in the original experiment (51, 400 and 600 items), rewritten for the new claim. Examples: "What kind of diet does Corinne Westbrook follow?", "I'm hosting a dinner party and Corinne Westbrook is coming. What main course should I cook for her? Explain briefly.", "Would Corinne Westbrook happily eat gummy bears or a gelatin dessert? Explain."
